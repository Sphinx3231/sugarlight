Blood sugar awareness

Blood sugar: what every adult should know.

Diabetes often develops for years without clear symptoms. High blood sugar quietly damages the heart, eyes, kidneys and nerves, and a simple blood test can find it early. Here is what to watch for, what your results mean, and how to lower your risk.

  • 589 million adults live with diabetes
  • 252 million are undiagnosed
  • Type 2 can often be prevented or delayed

What blood sugar is

Blood sugar, or glucose, comes from the food you eat. It is your body’s main source of energy. A hormone called insulin moves it out of your blood and into your cells.

  1. 1 · You eat

    Your body breaks carbohydrates down into glucose, which enters your blood. That’s why blood sugar rises after a meal.

  2. 2 · Insulin moves it

    Your pancreas releases insulin, which lets glucose into your cells. This brings blood sugar back down.

  3. 3 · Cells use it

    Your cells use glucose for energy: for your muscles, your heart and your brain.

What diabetes is

Diabetes means blood sugar stays too high because the body can’t make or use insulin properly.

Type 1: the pancreas makes little or no insulin. It can’t currently be prevented, and it is nobody’s fault.

Type 2: the body resists insulin or doesn’t make enough. It covers over 90% of people with diabetes, and it can often be prevented or delayed.

Gestational: high blood sugar that appears during pregnancy.

1 in 9

adults aged 20 to 79 live with diabetes worldwide.

Warning signs: too high or too low

It’s normal for blood sugar to rise after meals and come back down. Problems start when it stays too high, or drops too low.

Signs of high blood sugar

Also called hyperglycemia. Over time it damages nerves and blood vessels, and can lead to blindness, kidney failure, heart attack, stroke and amputation.

Common signs of diabetes:

  • Peeing often
  • Thirst and hunger
  • Weight loss without trying
  • Tiredness
  • Feeling irritable or moody
  • Blurry vision
  • Frequent infections
  • Slow-healing cuts
  • Numb or tingling hands or feet

Type 2 can grow slowly with few or no signs. Type 1 can appear suddenly, over weeks, with nausea, vomiting or stomach pain. That needs a doctor quickly.

Signs of low blood sugar

Also called hypoglycemia. Below 70 mg/dL is low, and below 55 mg/dL is severely low. People who use insulin or some diabetes medicines are most at risk.

Common signs:

  • Shaking
  • Sweating
  • Fast heartbeat
  • Nervousness
  • Confusion or irritability
  • Dizziness
  • Hunger

What to do for low blood sugar: the 15-15 rule

  1. 15

    Eat 15 grams of fast sugar. For example ½ cup of juice or regular soda, 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey, or 3 to 4 glucose tablets.

  2. 15

    Wait 15 minutes, then check blood sugar again.

  3. Still under 70? Repeat. Once back in range, eat a balanced snack.

If someone passes out or can’t swallow, call emergency services: a severe low needs glucagon and medical help. Don’t change diabetes medicines without talking to your doctor.

Your risk, and how to lower it

Type 2 diabetes can often be prevented or delayed. According to the CDC, lifestyle changes can cut the risk in half.

Are you at higher risk of type 2 diabetes?

According to the CDC, your risk of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes is higher if you:

  • Have overweight or obesity
  • Are 45 or older
  • Have a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes
  • Are physically active less than 3 times a week
  • Have non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
  • Have had gestational diabetes, or a baby who weighed over 9 pounds
  • Are African American, Hispanic or Latino, American Indian or Alaska Native (some Pacific Islander and Asian American people also have higher risk)

When to get tested

The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes in adults aged 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity.

If any of the risk factors apply to you, or you notice warning signs, ask your doctor about an A1C or fasting blood sugar test.

58%

lower risk of type 2 diabetes for people with prediabetes who join a lifestyle change program. For people over 60, it’s 71%.

CDC, National Diabetes Prevention Program

  • Move 150 minutes a week

    That’s about 30 minutes of brisk walking, 5 days a week.

  • Lose a little, if you need to

    For people with prediabetes and extra weight, losing 5 to 7% of body weight makes a real difference.

  • Eat well, skip tobacco

    A healthy diet and avoiding tobacco help prevent or delay type 2 diabetes, says WHO.

  • Get tested

    More than 4 in 10 adults with diabetes don’t know it. A simple blood test can tell you, so ask a doctor.

Supporting someone with diabetes

Partners, family and coworkers can help. Learn the signs of a low, know where they keep fast-acting sugar or glucagon, and know when to call emergency services.
